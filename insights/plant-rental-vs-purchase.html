Rent or own? The 24-month question

Evaluate asset ownership and recurring service on the same time horizon.

By Avesh Rawat · Partner, OneScape

ONESCAPE PERSPECTIVES

Avesh Rawat
Partner, OneScape

A corporate office can acquire plants and containers outright or use a rental arrangement with recurring horticulture care. Both models can work. The more useful question is which one suits the organisation's operating priorities over the period it expects to occupy and maintain the space.

A two-year view is often a practical starting point for examining the decision. It is long enough to capture routine maintenance and replacement exposure, without assuming the office will remain unchanged indefinitely.

Ownership makes sense when the organisation wants control of the assets.

Purchasing plants and planters can be appropriate for a stable office layout, especially where the facilities team already has an established maintenance arrangement. The assets belong to the organisation, and the initial specification can be treated as part of a wider interior fit-out.

But purchasing does not eliminate ongoing cost. Plants still need care. Some may need replacement. Containers may be damaged or relocated, and the facilities team must decide how these activities will be managed and funded.

Rental changes how the cost is distributed.

Under a rental arrangement, the supplier typically provides the plant assets and a recurring service model, with ownership and replacement obligations determined by the contract. This can reduce the need for an initial purchase and bring installation and upkeep into a more predictable monthly arrangement.

The important word is contract. Not every rental quotation includes the same services. Clients should check replacement allowances, damage exclusions, relocations, periodic refreshes and what happens when the agreement ends.

Compare the models on the same basis.

For a meaningful 24-month assessment, look at the total expected outlay for each approach:

  • Initial plants, planters, transport and installation
  • Routine maintenance over the contract period
  • Likely replacements and responsibilities
  • Changes in layout or additions to the inventory
  • Internal coordination and site-access requirements
  • End-of-term asset treatment and exit conditions

A lower upfront cost is not automatically a lower total cost. Equally, ownership is not inherently expensive. The right answer depends on the installation, the building and the organisation's preference for capital or operating expenditure.

Design decisions affect financial decisions.

Plant choice is part of the equation. A species that looks striking in a photograph but performs poorly under the site's lighting or air-conditioning may be the wrong investment under either model. Oversized containers, inaccessible planters and unnecessarily complex feature planting can raise the burden of upkeep.

A good proposal makes these trade-offs visible before the installation begins.

Choose the model that reduces uncertainty.

For some businesses, outright purchase and a separately managed AMC will be entirely sensible. Others will prefer a recurring rental arrangement that combines assets and service. The point is not to force a particular model, but to select one that the facilities and finance teams can live with comfortably.

Closing line: The best planting decision is the one that still makes sense after the novelty has worn off.
