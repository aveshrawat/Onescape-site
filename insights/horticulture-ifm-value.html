Where specialist horticulture fits in an IFM contract

A specialist service line can help an IFM partner broaden its offer without introducing delivery friction.

By Avesh Rawat · Partner, OneScape

ONESCAPE PERSPECTIVES

Avesh Rawat
Partner, OneScape

An integrated facilities contract brings many disciplines under one operating relationship. The client values the convenience of a single accountable partner; the IFM provider carries the responsibility of ensuring that specialist services meet their own standards without disrupting the wider contract.

Horticulture is a good example. It may represent a relatively modest part of overall facilities spend, but it is highly visible. Plants sit in reception areas, workspaces, circulation zones and landscaped approaches — exactly where employees, visitors and clients form impressions of the property.

That visibility is both an opportunity and an operating obligation.

A service line worth taking seriously.

For an IFM provider, horticulture can form part of an integrated bid, an existing contract extension or a separate scope introduced as the property evolves. A capable specialist partner may help the provider respond to these opportunities without creating an entirely new in-house operating discipline.

The commercial upside is not automatic. A service only contributes meaningfully if it is priced correctly, scoped responsibly and delivered with enough consistency to avoid eating into the margin through repeated intervention.

That is why the subcontracting decision should be about more than access to gardeners or plant suppliers.

The handover between teams matters.

In many arrangements, the IFM team owns the client relationship while the specialist is responsible for day-to-day horticulture. If expectations are not clear, the gaps become visible quickly: requests get passed between teams, replacements are delayed and minor issues turn into site escalations.

A sound working model should answer some basic questions at the outset. Who conducts the horticulture assessment? Who approves additional work? How are site changes communicated? What is the escalation route? Which items sit inside the agreed service fee, and which need separate approval?

These may not be glamorous questions, but they determine whether an otherwise attractive proposition will work after mobilisation.

Standardise the expectation, not every site.

Multi-site IFM contracts benefit from a shared service framework. It makes proposals easier to assess and helps stakeholders understand what they are buying. But an office in Bengaluru, a hospitality facility in Chennai and a landscaped property in Hyderabad will not have identical horticulture requirements.

The framework should therefore set common expectations for service, reporting and responsibility while allowing local planting and maintenance decisions to reflect the site.

The best partnership is almost invisible to the client.

The client should experience an integrated service, not the friction of several organisations trying to define where one scope ends and another begins.

For an IFM provider, that is the real value of specialist partnership: access to capability, a more complete offering and a manageable delivery arrangement. For the horticulture partner, it means understanding that technical expertise has to sit comfortably inside someone else's service commitment.

Closing line: A stronger offering starts with specialist knowledge. A stronger contract depends on how that knowledge is delivered.
