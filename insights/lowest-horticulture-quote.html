The lowest horticulture quote can be the most expensive

The monthly rate is only one part of the full cost of care.

By Avesh Rawat · Partner, OneScape

ONESCAPE PERSPECTIVES

Avesh Rawat
Partner, OneScape

Most horticulture tenders eventually come down to a price comparison. That is understandable. Procurement teams need a consistent basis for evaluation, and the lowest compliant offer often starts with an advantage.

The trouble is that horticulture contracts can look comparable on paper while carrying very different responsibilities.

One quotation may include regular visits, plant health checks and a defined replacement allowance. Another may exclude replacements, specify fewer visits or leave the treatment of declining plants open to interpretation. The difference may not become obvious until the contract is already running.

The number to examine is the total cost of care.

For indoor planting, the initial plant price is only one part of the expense. There may be planters, transportation, installation, seasonal care, replacements, relocations and the time the facility team spends coordinating service. External landscapes introduce other variables: soil condition, water availability, plant growth, weather exposure and the resources needed to maintain larger areas.

None of this means the highest quotation is necessarily the right one. It means the quotations should cover the same responsibility before the rates are compared.

A useful evaluation begins with five questions.

What exactly is included? The proposal should identify service areas, approximate inventory, visit frequency and the main tasks expected at every visit. A general promise to “maintain all plants” leaves too much room for interpretation.

Who carries replacement risk? Plants are living assets. Some decline because of maintenance; others because of light, air-conditioning, water exposure or changes to the premises. The contract should explain how the parties assess cause and assign responsibility.

How will the service fit the building? Access hours, security procedures, occupied office areas and the movement of materials can affect cost and execution. A supplier who has not considered these realities may be underpricing the work.

What happens when the space changes? Offices renovate. Furniture moves. Reception areas are redesigned. A good contract should make additions, removals and relocations manageable without turning every change into a dispute.

How will performance be reviewed? The client should know who to speak to, how issues are recorded and how recurring problems are resolved. Good service is not only about the scheduled visit; it is also about the response between visits.

Better procurement begins with a better brief.

The simplest way to improve a horticulture tender is to make the scope more precise. Include the plant or area inventory where possible. State the expected standard, define replacement terms and ask suppliers to identify exclusions clearly.

Then compare the cost over a realistic contract period, not just the first invoice.

A lower monthly fee may still be the best value. But it should win because the operating model is sound, not because important work has been left out of the quotation.

Closing line: A well-written horticulture contract protects the budget by making the work understandable.
