The overlooked revenue line

What if horticulture were a better-designed service line, not a pass-through expense?

The overlooked revenue line.

The decision scenario

An IFM provider is extending an enterprise contract. The client wants reception planting, indoor care and external landscape support added to the scope. The horticulture requirement is not large relative to the main FM contract, so there is a temptation to treat it as a simple pass-through purchase.

Yet the provider is accountable for delivery, escalations, site coordination and the client relationship — even when the actual gardening work is subcontracted.

Where value gets lost

If scope, replacement responsibility or resource assumptions are vague, a nominally profitable service line can consume more management time than expected. Sites request additional visits, replacements are disputed and small variations go unpriced. The problem is not that horticulture is inherently unattractive; the commercial structure is incomplete.

A better model

Define the service package before pricing it: inventory, service frequency, reporting, replacement rules, site access, variation handling and escalation ownership. Make specialist inputs available at bid stage, not only after the contract has been won. Separate recurring work from one-off improvements and establish clear approvals for change orders.

The commercial insight

Horticulture can support additional contract scope, stronger integrated bids and predictable delivery when the specialist partner understands both the horticultural requirement and the IFM operating model. That potential depends on disciplined scoping, not on a promised margin percentage.

Questions to ask

What part of the horticulture brief is already funded? Which deliverables are chargeable additions? Who owns replacements? Can new sites enter the same service framework? How are urgent works authorised? Can the specialist deliver under the IFM team's reporting expectations?
