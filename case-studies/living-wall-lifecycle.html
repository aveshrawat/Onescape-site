When a living wall becomes a liability.

The business situation

A prominent vertical garden is intended to become an architectural feature within a commercial property. It has to meet a visual brief, accommodate installation constraints and continue looking presentable after the contractors leave.

OneScape's green infrastructure work involves the interface between planting, installation and future maintenance — an interface that is easy to underestimate during project procurement.

What often gets overlooked

A wall may look excellent in the presentation drawing while leaving difficult questions unanswered. How is the upper planting accessed? Where does excess water go? Who inspects irrigation? What happens when an individual module fails? Can replacement plants be moved through the operating building without disruption?

These are not minor maintenance details. They affect lifecycle cost, access planning and the risk of an expensive correction after handover.

The approach

Treat maintainability as part of the design brief. Review plant selection, system interfaces, water movement, access, installation sequencing and the operating team's role before final approval. When decisions have to be made during execution, consider how each will affect the property over time.

The procurement insight

Before awarding a vertical garden contract, require clarity on what happens after practical completion. A cheaper installation may leave a more expensive recurring obligation if access, replacements or water management have been under-specified.

What this story demonstrates

Green infrastructure is not simply a visual product. It is a constructed system with living components. The strongest outcome is one that the facility team can realistically own and maintain.
